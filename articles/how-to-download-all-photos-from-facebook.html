How to Download All Photos from Facebook

There is a specific kind of panic that hits when your phone dies and you realize the only copy of your kid's first birthday is sitting on someone else's server. Facebook has spent fifteen years quietly becoming the family photo album for a few billion people, and most of them have never once asked for the files back.

By PhotoMorning StaffSeptember 25, 2026

There is a specific kind of panic that hits when your phone dies and you realize the only copy of your kid's first birthday is sitting on someone else's server. Facebook has spent fifteen years quietly becoming the family photo album for a few billion people, and most of them have never once asked for the files back.

The good news: Facebook will hand over the whole archive, and it is one of the few genuinely useful things the platform still does. The bad news: the tool is buried three menus deep, the download link expires in four days, and what you get back is not quite what you uploaded.

This guide covers the bulk export, the single-photo route, the limits Facebook applies without telling you, and what to do with the files once they are yours again.

Two Different Jobs, Two Different Tools

Almost every frustrated post on this topic comes from people using the wrong tool for the job they actually had in mind.

If you want everything, meaning every photo you ever posted plus your posts, comments, messages and friend list, you want the export tool in account settings. It builds a ZIP file in the background and emails you when it is ready.

If you want two photos from a trip in 2019, the export is absurd overkill. You wait hours for a multi-gigabyte file so you can dig out two JPEGs. Just open the photo and download it directly.

Decide which one you are doing before you start clicking. The export cannot be narrowed to "just that weekend in Lisbon" without also committing to a date range and waiting anyway.

Requesting the Full Export

On a desktop browser:

  • Click your profile picture in the top right, then Settings & privacy, then Settings.
  • Choose Meta Account in the sidebar, then Your information and permissions.
  • Click Export your information, then Create export.
  • Pick the profile you want to export from.
  • Choose Export to device.
  • Set the options that matter: what to include, a date range, the format, and media quality.

Older accounts still show this as Your Facebook Information then Download Your Information. Same tool, older label. If you cannot find either, type "download your information" into the settings search box and Facebook will walk you to it.

On mobile, the path runs through the menu button (three lines, top right on Android, bottom right on iOS) into Settings & privacy then Settings, then Your information and permissions. The menus are identical, the scrolling is worse.

Pick Photos and videos rather than everything. A full account export drags along years of comment history, ad interactions and off-Facebook activity logs. That can be tens of gigabytes and it takes far longer to prepare. If photos are what you came for, untick the rest under "Specific types of information."

The Settings That Actually Matter

Date range. All time if you want a real backup, last year if you are clearing recent material. There is no filter for "only photos where I am tagged," which is annoying, but you can sort the ZIP contents afterward.

Format. HTML gives you a browsable index that opens in any browser, with your posts laid out roughly as they appeared, plus folders of media. JSON is machine-readable and better if you plan to feed the archive into another tool. For most people HTML is the right call.

Media quality. Higher means larger files and a longer wait. It is the only quality lever Facebook offers, so take it.

Facebook then builds the archive in the background. Small accounts take minutes. Accounts with fifteen years of photos and videos can take a day or more.

The Four-Day Window Is Real

When the export finishes, Facebook notifies you by email, or the file appears under Available downloads in the export tool. It stays there for four days. After that Facebook deletes it and you have to request the entire thing again, which means queueing behind the same processing job you just waited through.

Save it to a drive with room to spare, not to a laptop that is nearly full. And do not do this on hotel wifi.

Downloading One Photo, or a Handful

Open the photo, click the three dots in the corner, then Download. On mobile the same option appears as a downward arrow. Facebook saves the largest version it currently serves, which is not the same as the version you uploaded.

For a small batch, open your Photos section, tick the images you want, then choose Download from the menu. There is no select-all in the grid, and the one-click "download album" button has been gone since 2018.

What You Will Not Get Back

The export gives you your photographs as Facebook has stored them, which in several respects is not the same file you uploaded.

Your EXIF data is gone. Facebook strips camera metadata on upload: no lens, no aperture, no shutter speed, no GPS coordinates, no capture timestamp beyond whatever Facebook recorded itself. If you were hoping to rebuild a timeline from the files, the dates reflect uploads rather than the moment the shutter fired.

Resolution is capped. Facebook recompresses everything. The "higher quality" setting returns a better version than the standard render, but it is not a guarantee of your original file. Photos uploaded before Facebook raised its size limits can come back noticeably smaller than what you sent in 2012.

It is a backup, not a restore. You cannot import that archive into a new Facebook account, and there is no supported way to rebuild a profile from an export. What you have is a folder of files and an HTML index, which beats nothing but is worth understanding before you delete an account.

Tagged photos of you are not yours to keep. The export includes photos you posted and photos of you that you can currently see. If the owner later deletes their account or tightens their privacy settings, your archive copy may be all that survives. There is no way to bulk-download another person's album through Facebook, and the third-party scrapers that promise it break the platform's terms, frequently harvest your login, and are worth avoiding entirely.

Sending It Somewhere Else Instead

You do not have to download anything at all. The same tool offers Export to external service, which transfers directly to a destination such as Google Photos, and it can be scheduled to run every month or two instead of once.

For an archive that keeps itself current, that is the better option. For a final copy before you close the account, download the ZIP and keep it somewhere you control.

What to Do With the Files Once They Are Yours

Unzip the archive and you will find albums as folders plus the index page. The folder names are Facebook's, so "Mobile uploads" and a date stamp is about as descriptive as it gets.

Sort before you do anything else. Pull out the images worth keeping, rename them by date and subject, drop the rest. Most people discover that 80 percent of their archive is screenshots, memes and near-identical duplicates, which is exactly the mess that makes a phone-based photo collection impossible to search. Digital housekeeping is dull, but a folder structure you can navigate is the difference between a backup and a landfill. The habit is worth building regardless of where you post, because [photography culture is not shrinking](https://photomorning.com/articles/photography-isnt-dying-vsco.html). The average person simply makes more images than any filing system was designed for.

Keep two local copies on separate drives and one offsite, either in cloud storage or on a drive at another address. Open the archive once a year to confirm it still works. Drives fail quietly, and you do not want to learn that at the moment you need the files.

Then print something. Ten or twenty of the best frames, on paper, in a drawer. The reason to pull your photos out of Facebook is not only distrust of the platform. A file nobody ever looks at is not really a memory yet.

Tips for a Clean Export

  • Start the export when you will be near your computer for a day, not the night before you travel.
  • Take the higher media quality setting and accept the wait.
  • Choose HTML unless you have a specific tool waiting for JSON.
  • Download the ZIP before the four-day window closes, and set a reminder.
  • Schedule an export to an external service for ongoing backup, and download a full ZIP separately if you plan to close the account.
  • Before deleting anything, search the archive for the few photos you would genuinely be upset to lose. That is the whole point.

Conclusion

Facebook's export tool is clunky, slow, and capped at a resolution you did not choose, but it works and it is free. Start the request, take the better quality setting, and use those four days to get the file onto a drive you own.

The wider lesson is worth sitting with. Photos shared on a social platform are lent to you, not owned, whether the platform is Facebook, Instagram or whatever replaces them. The pressure runs in both directions, too: some venues now push back on phone photography in the name of experience and safety, as with the [smartphone bans spreading through India's tiger reserves](https://photomorning.com/articles/india-tiger-reserves-smartphone-ban.html). Platforms treat your images as raw material, and [YouTube's expansion of likeness detection tools](https://photomorning.com/articles/youtube-ai-clone-detection.html) for creators shows how far that logic now reaches. If a photo matters, it belongs somewhere you can hold onto it.

FAQ

How long does a Facebook photo export take?

Usually between ten minutes and a few hours. Accounts with years of video and tens of thousands of photos can run past a day. Facebook emails you when the archive is ready, and you can watch progress under Available downloads in the export tool.

Why did my Facebook download link stop working?

The link stays live for four days. After that, Facebook deletes the file and you must create a new export. Request it again and download promptly this time.

Does the export include photos I am tagged in but did not post?

Yes, as long as you can currently see them. Photos you have been untagged from, or that sit at a privacy level you cannot access, are not included.

Can I download an entire album a friend posted?

No. Facebook offers no bulk download for other people's content. You can save individual photos the owner has left downloadable, and nothing more. Scraping tools that advertise otherwise break the platform's terms and are a common source of hijacked accounts.

Will the downloaded photos keep their camera metadata?

No. Facebook strips EXIF data on upload, so lens, aperture, shutter speed and GPS information are lost. What you get back is the image without the technical record of how it was made.

Can I restore my Facebook account from the export?

No. The archive is a copy of your content, not a restorable image of your profile. Facebook does not support importing it into a new account.
